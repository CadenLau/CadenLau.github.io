In development · Limited disclosureThis is an unreleased Game Neighbor LLC project. To protect our shared intellectual property, this case study describes my role and design objectives without publishing proprietary rules, cards, artwork, balance data, or other confidential material.

Design objectives

The game features 25 playable characters and a deck of support cards. Players build teams through drafting and make combat decisions around character abilities, resource management, and interactions between cards. The design aims to reward strategic thinking, adaptation, and counterplay rather than relying on a single dominant strategy.

A central challenge is making individual characters feel distinct while ensuring their abilities remain interesting in the context of different teammates and opponents. Drafting should matter, but it should not eliminate meaningful decisions during combat.

My contributions

  • Co-design a roster of characters and support cards with distinct abilities, intended roles, and potential team synergies.
  • Develop interconnected drafting, team composition, combat, resource management, and simultaneous action-resolution systems.
  • Refine abilities, card interactions, and rules through iterative prototyping and playtesting, with attention to strategic depth, clarity, and counterplay.
  • Build simulation and search tools to evaluate character matchups and gameplay strategies beyond what is practical through manual testing alone.

A quantitative approach to balance

I built a Python simulation engine that models drafting and combat, alongside Monte Carlo tree search (MCTS) agents that can evaluate possible decisions. I have used the engine to analyze 10,000 simulated matches, including mirrored matchups to support comparisons between teams and strategies.

The goal is to investigate questions such as whether particular team compositions consistently outperform others, how card interactions affect matchups, and whether a balance change creates unexpected effects elsewhere in the system. Simulation is a complement to—not a replacement for—human feedback. It can measure aspects of a game’s behavior, but it cannot decide whether a mechanic feels satisfying, intuitive, or fun.

Design & prototype→Playtest & simulate→Evaluate & iterate
